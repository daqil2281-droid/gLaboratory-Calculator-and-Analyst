Quick physics guide

Gravity: a simple pendulum has approximately T² = (4π²/g)l for small angles. SHM: the restoring force makes the bob oscillate around equilibrium. Energy: a moving bob can lose mechanical energy to air resistance and its support; the heat panel is an illustrative model, not a measured laboratory result.

How does the graph give g?

For a simple pendulum at small angles, T = 2π√(l/g). Squaring gives T² = (4π²/g)l, which has the straight-line form y = mx through the origin. Here y = T², x = l, and the gradient m = 4π²/g, so g = 4π²/m.

The centroid is the mean point (mean l, mean T²). This display draws the origin-to-centroid line so it visibly passes through both points; its gradient is mean(T²)/mean(l). Real measurements may scatter around this line. For the most defensible experimental estimate, compare with an unconstrained least-squares fit and discuss uncertainty and systematic error.

Heat dissipation: moving vs static pendulum

This conceptual model compares a bob initially warmer than its surroundings. A swinging bob is assigned a slightly larger effective heat-transfer coefficient to illustrate additional airflow. Real heat loss depends on bob shape, material, airflow, temperature difference, and support friction; motion does not guarantee a fixed extra cooling rate in every setup.
